# 3. Emotion representations

- **Categorical** outputs use named labels, such as anger, joy, or sadness. A dataset can also define a neutral class. [S01](../sources/S01.md), §2, pp. 2–3.
- **Dimensional** outputs use ratings on scales. Valence describes pleasantness, and arousal describes activation or intensity. Some representations add dominance, a dimension related to control. [S01](../sources/S01.md), §2.1, p. 3.

**Illustrative example.** Two imagined clips could both receive high-arousal ratings even if one sounds pleasant and the other unpleasant. These are hypothetical judgments.

The review reports that listeners can interpret the same speech differently and that the available response categories can influence their judgments. It also describes a lack of consensus on a comprehensive representation of emotion. [S01](../sources/S01.md), §§2–2.1, pp. 2–3.

See [Applications and limitations](ser-limitations.md) for what a prediction can support.

## Example from RAVDESS

Actors performed specified emotion categories. Listeners then chose an emotion category and separately rated strength and genuineness. The available categories and the actor’s intended category are part of this study’s design. The authors identify valence and arousal ratings as future work. [S03](../sources/S03.md), Description of RAVDESS files, p. 11, Validation of RAVDESS stimuli, p. 12, and Discussion, p. 23.

See [Datasets and evaluation](../datasets/datasets-and-evaluation.md) for the study setup.

## Example from IEMOCAP

IEMOCAP annotators could select several categories for a turn. The original study reports a unique categorical winner under its voting procedure for 74.6% of turns. It also collected activation, valence, and dominance assessments. [S04](../sources/S04.md), §5.2, manuscript pp. 12–15 and 17–19.
